Plain-language guide

Proof, not promises

An AI will tell you the job is done. It will sound certain. Sometimes it is wrong. This is a system built on the opposite assumption — that nobody's word counts as evidence, including the AI's.

The problem

Why this exists

Imagine hiring an electrician. They spend the afternoon at your house, pack up, and say "all done, everything's working." They sound confident. They usually are right.

You still get it inspected. Not because you think they're lying, but because "I did it" and "it has been checked" are different claims, and only one of them keeps your house from burning down.

Today's AI is the confident electrician. Ask it to do a job and it will report success in fluent, reassuring prose — whether or not it actually succeeded. It has no way of knowing the difference, because to the AI, producing a convincing report is the job.

The whole idea in one line

This system is the inspector. It gives work to an AI, then refuses to accept the answer until something independent has checked it.

The core idea

Who is allowed to decide what

Most AI tools put the AI in charge of everything — what to do, when it's finished, whether to try again. That's the mistake. Here, the AI is a very capable worker who is never also the supervisor.

Ordinary computer code — predictable, testable, boring in the best way — holds the clipboard. The AI does the thinking. The split looks like this:

The AI decides

  • What the request actually means
  • How to break it into steps
  • How to do each step
  • Judgement calls where there's no right answer

The code decides

  • What runs, and in what order
  • What the AI is allowed to touch
  • Whether the work passed
  • When to stop, retry, or ask you
  • How much it's allowed to cost

That division is why the system can make firm promises instead of hopeful ones. A supervisor made of code doesn't get talked round.

The leash

What it is allowed to touch

"The code decides what the AI can touch" is the sort of line every product says. Here is what it means concretely, because the detail is the entire value.

Nothing is permitted unless you permitted it. Not "nothing dangerous" — nothing. A worker that hasn't been granted the ability to read files cannot read files, however reasonably it asks, however it phrases the request, and whatever a web page it just read tried to tell it to do. Its request is a suggestion, handed to a separate piece of code that holds the actual keys.

Files
One folder, and no way out of it. Paths that climb out with .. or slip out through a shortcut are refused — and the check runs on where the path really lands, not on how it looks.
Websites
A list of addresses you wrote by hand. Not "anything except known-bad sites"; an empty list means it reaches nothing at all. If a permitted site forwards it elsewhere, the new address is checked too, at every hop.
Programs
Switched off entirely by default, and once switched on, limited to exact programs you named. It doesn't get a general-purpose command line unless you deliberately override that, because handing something a command line is permission to run everything.
Secrets
Removed before the AI sees them, and removed again before anything is written down. Passwords and keys live in your machine's environment; the settings file only ever names them.
Your say-so
Some steps stop and wait for a person. The job doesn't guess, and doesn't time out into a decision — it waits, in the same durable record as the rest of the work, until somebody answers.
The part that matters most

Every refusal is written down with its reason. So "did it try to do something it shouldn't have?" is a question with an answer you can go and read — not a reassurance you have to take on trust.

How a job runs

What happens when you ask for something

You give it a goal in plain English — not a recipe. You don't say how. Working out the how is its job. Here's the whole sequence, and it genuinely is a sequence, so the numbers mean something.

It works out what you actually asked for

It separates what you stated from what it's guessing, and writes both down separately so a guess can never quietly become a fact later.

It sizes up the job

A simple question gets one worker and one check. A tangled request gets a proper plan. It reads the shape of your request, not the topic.

A one-line question genuinely does get handled in one step — it won't invent committee meetings for "what time is it".

It writes a plan

A list of steps, which ones depend on which, and — crucially — how each one will be checked. The plan is a proper structured document, not a paragraph of intentions.

It checks the plan is possible before starting

If a step needs a tool that doesn't exist, you find out now, with a clear reason — not three steps in with a baffling error.

It does the work

Independent steps run at the same time. Steps that touch the same thing are made to queue, so two workers never trample each other. Each worker gets only the tools its own step needs — and can't even see the rest.

Every step gets checked

Not by the worker who did it. By something separate: running your tests, matching a required format, confirming a file exists. If the check fails, the step failed — no matter how confidently the worker reported success.

When something breaks, it responds sensibly

A timeout gets retried. A bad result gets a different approach. A refused permission gets escalated to you, never worked around. Every route ends either in success or in asking a human — never in an endless loop.

It reports honestly

Including admitting when it can't prove something. That last part is the point of the whole exercise.

Reading the answer

Two words, not one

Every finished job reports what happened and how much to believe it. Most people look only at the first. The second is where the value is.

Confirmed

Something independent checked it and it passed. This is proof.

Likely

It passed, but judgement was involved. Probably right. Not proven.

Uncertain

The work got done, but nothing could verify it. An honest shrug — and far more useful than a false tick.

Blocked

It stopped and is waiting on something outside its control — usually you.

Failed

It did not achieve the goal, and it says so plainly.

The bit people misread

"Completed" plus "uncertain" is not a bug. It means the work was done and the system could not prove it. Most AI tools would have shown you a green tick there. This one tells you the truth, which is less satisfying and much more useful.

To turn uncertain into confirmed, give it a check it can actually run — your test suite, a required format, a file that must exist.

The words

Nine words worth knowing

The documentation uses these constantly. They're simpler than they sound.

Objective
What you asked for. One sentence of plain English. The only thing you're required to provide.
Task
One step of the plan. Written to stand alone, so whoever picks it up needs no other context.
Agent
A worker. Not a person and not a personality — a job description saying what it can do and what it's allowed to touch.
Tool
An action it can take. Read a file, run a command, call a website. Without tools it can only think and talk.
Capability
A kind of ability. Tasks say what they need; workers say what they offer; the system matches them. Nobody is picked by name.
Validator
The inspector. The thing that decides whether a step really passed. The most valuable thing you can configure.
Policy
The rules about what may happen. Consulted before every action. It answers yes or no, and its no is final — nothing the AI writes can talk it round.
Approval
A stop-and-ask. For steps you'd rather a person signed off. The job waits instead of guessing.
Skill
House rules. Written guidance handed to a worker — how you like things done. It advises; it never grants permission.
Using it

Getting it running

Four things, in order. The second is the one people skip and then wonder why nothing works.

1 · Install it

# from the project folder
pip install -e ".[all]"
orchestrator init .

2 · Give it a brain

It can plan without an AI model connected, but it cannot do anything. It won't pretend otherwise — it stops and asks you. Point it at a model in .orchestrator/config.yaml. Local models are free and private; hosted ones are sharper. 

models:
  providers:
    - type: ollama          # runs on your machine, no key
      base_url: http://localhost:11434

Then confirm it's actually reachable, rather than assuming: 

orchestrator validate   # is the setup sane?
orchestrator health     # is the model actually answering?

3 · Ask for something

orchestrator run "Compare the three files in ./notes
                  and list where they disagree."

Say what you want, not how to get it. If you want a particular shape of work, spelling out the parts as a list nudges it toward a real plan.

4 · Read what it did

orchestrator inspect <id>   # the plan, and what passed
orchestrator audit   <id>   # every decision, in order

audit is the one to open when the result surprises you. Every choice the system made is in there, in sequence, with its reason — including the ones that led somewhere you didn't expect.

Before your first real job

Set a budget. It's counted before the spend, not discovered after, so it can't run away with your money or your afternoon. 

limits:
  max_model_calls: 50
  max_wall_seconds: 600
  max_cost: 2.00
Not just you

When other people start using it

Running something on your own laptop and running it for other people are different jobs. This treats them as different jobs, rather than trusting you to remember to tighten everything afterwards.

The one setting to understand

A line called profile says who a copy is for: development, internal-pilot, or production. Leaving it out means production — the strictest of the three, not the friendliest.

That is deliberate, and it is the reverse of the usual arrangement. The classic accident is convenient defaults quietly reaching real users because nobody realised they were the development ones. Here, forgetting gets you the careful setting, and startup tells you plainly which one you are in.

Three other things change once it isn't only you:

  • Everyone gets their own key, and every key has a job description. Reading work, starting work, answering approvals, administering — each granted separately. The key your status dashboard uses cannot start anything.
  • A leaked key can be switched off immediately. Revoking it takes hold across every running copy at once: no restart, and no window in which it still quietly works.
  • Nothing is exposed to the network directly. The published setup puts a dedicated gatekeeper in front to handle encryption, and the orchestrator opens no public door of its own.

None of that is aspiration. A script in the repository starts the real multi-copy arrangement, revokes a key mid-flight, tries to reach a forbidden address, and attempts to walk in wearing a forged identity — then fails loudly if any of it behaves other than as described.

Is it for you

When to reach for it — and when not to

It's deliberately not built for one industry. What decides whether it suits your job isn't the subject, it's the shape.

Good fit
  • Work that breaks into separate pieces
  • Anything with a real test at the end
  • Jobs where a wrong answer is expensive
  • Batches of many similar items
  • Long jobs you need to pause and resume
  • Anything needing an audit trail or sign-off
Poor fit
  • Back-and-forth conversation
  • One quick question — just ask a model
  • Purely creative work with no way to check it
  • Anything needing an answer in seconds
  • Thousands of steps at once
  • Pipelines you've already automated properly
The honest version

This is a supervised way to run AI work, not a faster one. If you don't need the record, the sign-off gates, the spending ceiling, and the refusal to overstate what was proven — a plain AI assistant is less machinery for the same output. The governance is the product.

What building it taught us about trusting AI

The system worked perfectly against a fake, predictable model. Pointing it at real ones broke it repeatedly — and every break was instructive.

Asked to read a file, one model invented the contents rather than admit it had no way to open one. The tools existed; the planner had no way to request them. It filled the gap with fluent, confident, entirely fictional text.

Another was told its work would be checked against a required format — but was never shown the format. It failed a test it had no way to read. A check the worker can't see is a trap, not a check.

A third spent its entire thinking budget thinking, produced nothing at all, and the system reported that empty silence as a successful answer.

None of these appeared in hundreds of automated tests. All appeared within minutes of using a real model on a real file. Which is, fittingly, the entire argument for building the thing this way.

In short

The one thing to remember

Anyone can say the work is finished. This is a system that goes and looks — and that tells you plainly when it couldn't.

Everything else — the plans, the workers, the permissions, the budgets, the audit trail — exists to make that one sentence true.
